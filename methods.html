How the atlas finds, judges and links the research

The atlas is run as a living systematic review: fixed rules for what counts, repeatable searches, every decision logged in public, and an explicit confidence rating for each link.

The goal

Links each resting-state EEG pattern (which band, higher or lower, where on the scalp) to the disorders and symptoms it has been reported in, with the confidence the research supports for each link.

Every rule below serves that goal. Treatment (neurofeedback) studies are a secondary layer, shown for the same patterns.

The question

For each disorder and each of its core symptoms: which resting-state EEG band-power differences have been reported, at which scalp sites, in which direction, and how confident can we be? As a second question: which EEG feedback (neurofeedback) treatments target those patterns, and how well did they hold up?

In structured form
  • Population: participants with a diagnosed disorder, or with a measured symptom (e.g. inattention, sleep difficulty)
  • Measure: resting-state EEG band power (delta, theta, alpha, SMR, beta, high beta, gamma), ratios and asymmetry
  • Comparison: healthy controls, or symptom severity within a group
  • Result recorded: band, direction (higher / lower / asymmetric), scalp sites, size of the study and strength of the design

What counts

EEG markers: included

  • Resting recordings only: awake, eyes open or eyes closed, no task
  • Full clinical qEEG and simple EEG (one or a few channels) both count
  • Reports the band and the direction of the difference, and the sites or that the result is whole-head
  • Meta-analyses, case-control studies, cohorts, and studies correlating EEG with symptom severity

EEG markers: excluded

  • Task-based EEG, event-related or evoked responses (ERP, steady-state responses) and sleep EEG
  • Case reports, animal studies, and papers without enough detail to record a band and direction
  • Papers that mix conditions are kept, but only their resting results are used

Treatment studies

  • Neurofeedback or other EEG feedback training in humans, with the protocol stated (band, training direction, sites)
  • Any design is accepted; design, control type and blinding are recorded so the confidence rating can account for them

Finding studies: how we try to catch everything

No single database or search finds every relevant study. In a study of 58 published reviews, 16% of included references were found in only one database, and an estimated 60% of reviews did not reach 95% recall; Embase, MEDLINE, Web of Science and Google Scholar together performed best [Bramer 2017]. So the atlas layers several methods:

  1. Database searchesPubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, PsycINFO and Google Scholar, using the same structured query for every disorder (below). For treatment trials, also Cochrane CENTRAL and ClinicalTrials.gov, to find unpublished or negative trials.
  2. Backward citation chasingEvery meta-analysis and review in the atlas is a seed: its reference list is screened for primary studies we do not yet have.
  3. Forward citation chasingFor each key paper, newer papers that cite it are screened. This catches studies published after the seed reviews.
  4. Benchmark checkA set of known, important papers (the meta-analyses already in the atlas) must be found by the database search. If the search misses any, the query is broadened and re-run. This is the main test of whether the search is catching everything.
  5. Monthly alertsSaved searches run again each month, and each run is recorded with its counts.
  6. SuggestionsAnyone can propose a paper through the "Suggest a study" form; it goes through the same screening.

The core query

Three blocks are combined: EEG terms, resting-state terms and spectral terms, plus the disorder. Exclusion words are applied at screening rather than in the query, so that relevant papers are not lost by accident.

(electroencephalography[MeSH] OR EEG OR qEEG OR electroencephalogram)
AND (resting OR "eyes closed" OR "eyes open")
AND (power OR spectral OR spectrum OR asymmetry OR theta OR alpha OR delta OR beta OR gamma OR oscillations)
AND humans[MeSH] AND [disorder MeSH term]

This is version 2. Version 1 failed the benchmark check: it missed two known papers whose abstracts say "theta activity" or "alpha oscillations" instead of "power", so band names and "oscillations" were added. Version 2 finds all six benchmark papers.

A second query targets direct symptom links (EEG correlated with symptom ratings), and a third targets treatment trials. The exact strings are kept in search-protocol.md.

Screening

  1. Log every hitEach paper found is added to the research log as "to review", with its citation and where it was found.
  2. Title and abstractClear misses are excluded with a short reason (e.g. "task-based", "sleep EEG", "MEG only").
  3. Full textThe rest are read in full. A paper is included only if band, direction and sites can be checked in the paper itself.
  4. Second checkScreening and data extraction are done by an AI model following a written protocol. A frozen random sample of these decisions is checked independently by human reviewers, and agreement is reported (in progress).

Numbers at each stage are reported in the style of a PRISMA flow diagram, the standard for reporting systematic reviews [Page 2021].

How confident are we?

Pooled results. The atlas lookup ranks each disorder by a direction-based synthesis of the full-text studies (vote counting with a sign test, GRADE certainty, sensitivity analyses). Abstract-only studies are listed but not pooled. Full protocol: synthesis protocol v1.0 · results table.

Each link (for example, "theta higher in ADHD") gets a rating from High to Very low. The steps follow the GRADE approach used in clinical guidelines, which separates quality of evidence into four levels [Guyatt 2008], simplified for this atlas:

  1. Start from the best supporting designHigh: meta-analysis or randomised trial · Moderate: controlled study or cohort · Low: case-control or narrative review · Very low: uncontrolled
  2. Rate down for inconsistencyOne level if any study finds the opposite direction or fails to replicate.
  3. Rate down for thin evidenceOne level if the support is a single primary study.
  4. Rate down for indirectnessOne level in the symptom view when studies compared diagnosed groups but did not measure the symptom itself.
  5. Cap for treatmentsIf no blinded or sham-controlled trial supports a treatment, its rating cannot go above Low.

How disorders, symptoms and EEG patterns are linked

  • Disorder → EEG pattern: for each band, the direction reported by most supporting studies is shown; contested studies are listed but do not set the direction.
  • Disorder → symptoms: each study is tagged with the core symptoms of the group studied (markers) or the outcomes it measured (treatments).
  • Symptom → EEG pattern: either direct (the study correlated EEG with symptom severity) or indirect (through the diagnosis). Direct links are the priority for new searches, because they are what the atlas currently lacks.
  • Neighbouring electrodes: scalp electrodes pick up activity from nearby cortex through volume conduction, so findings at adjacent sites are shown, and labelled, when an area is selected [Srinivasan 1998].

Keeping it up to date

The atlas is a living systematic review: it is updated continually as new evidence appears, rather than redone from scratch every few years [Elliott 2017]. All data live in the GitHub repository, so every change is dated and can be traced:

  • research-log.csv: every paper considered, with its status and reason
  • research.js: the included studies, with all extracted fields
  • search-protocol.md: the exact searches and a dated record of each run

Known limits

  • Methods differ between studies: eyes open versus closed, reference choice, absolute versus relative power and band boundaries all change results.
  • Patterns overlap across disorders: a review of 184 resting-state studies found that band changes are rarely unique to one disorder [Newson 2019]. A pattern in one person never confirms a diagnosis.
  • Publication bias: positive findings are more likely to be published; trial registries and forward citation chasing reduce but do not remove this.
  • Age, medication and comorbidity affect the resting EEG and are not yet recorded systematically.
  • AI-assisted collection: every included citation is checked, but summaries should still be verified against the original paper.

How to contribute

Suggest a study or give feedback through the GitHub issue forms (a free GitHub account is needed). Each suggestion is logged and screened against the rules above.
